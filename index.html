Where you stand

The method

One page. Print it — it should outlive the app.

Taking a verse in

Hear it first. Three times, before you ever say it. Your first reading sets the phrasing you keep for decades, so don't invent it.

Break at clauses, 3–7 words. Never at arbitrary word counts. Grammar is free scaffolding.

Write the intention, not the topic. Not "about grace" — "Paul reassuring people who think they've been abandoned." Once you hold the intention, the exact words start to feel necessary rather than arbitrary.

Same rhythm, every time. Same stresses, same pauses. You don't need a melody; you need consistency.

The ladder

0 — hear it recited
1 — text open, then hide and say it
2 — first letters only
3 — quarter blanked, middle first
4 — most blanked
5 — reference alone
6 — dropped in mid-verse, carry on

Up a rung after two clean passes. Down a rung after two fails — but only while it's still new. Once it's settled, stay high and let it be hard.

Practising off the clock

Open any verse from the Verses tab and tap Practise now. Full drill, full scoring, full correction — recorded nowhere.

That last part is deliberate rather than lazy. Reviewing something while you still remember it well builds almost no durability, and letting those reps into the schedule would pull your intervals down and cost you reviews for years. Look at a verse whenever you like; just don't let it move the clock.

The address

Knowing a verse you can't place is a real failure, and a common one. The words have grammar, rhythm and an argument holding them up. "Romans 8:28" has none of that — it's an arbitrary label, so it fades faster than the verse does.

Say the address as the first line. Same rhythm, every time, so it stops being a separate fact and becomes word order — which is the thing memory is best at. This only helps in one direction, though: serial chains don't run backwards, so it makes the address available when you start from it, not retrievable when you start from the text.

For that direction you need a hook, which is why the app offers one only after a number has actually started slipping.

So it's asked in the other direction, on its own clock: you get the opening line, you give the address. Near misses count for something — two verses out scores better than the wrong chapter.

When you miss

Hear the line. Say it reading along. Then type it alone and have it checked — an unverified "say it to yourself" step is the same self-grading that lets wording quietly erode.

Then it comes back before the session ends. That last part is the one everybody skips, and it's worth more than the other three.

Six ways it fails

Middle sag — edges solid, middle mush. Drill the middle alone.
Cold start — fluent from word one, helpless from the middle. Practise entering mid-verse.
Lost reference — you know it, can't place it. Drill text→reference.
Drift — meaning kept, wording eroded. Never grade yourself.
Fluency illusion — feels known, isn't. Check the calibration page.
Room-bound — works at your desk, fails in conversation. Move around.

The rules that hold it together

Nothing is ever finished. Dropping a verse once you know it is how you lose it. Retrieval keeps it; re-reading doesn't.

Two new verses a day. No more, ever. A good week at ten a day builds a debt that arrives in three weeks and ends the project.

Say it to a person weekly. Every tradition that actually works at this is oral and social. This is the part the app can't do for you.

Hooks for numbers

A personal association beats an invented one every time — a birthday, a house number, an age. Use those wherever they exist.

Where they don't, the app builds one from the Major System, which is four centuries old and still what competitive memorisers use. Digits become consonant sounds; vowels are free, so any number turns into a word:

1 t, d   2 n   3 m   4 r   5 l
6 sh, ch, j   7 k, g   8 f, v   9 p, b   0 s, z

So 8 is ivy, 28 is knife, and Romans 8:28 becomes an ivy-wrapped knife. You don't have to learn the system to use what it produces — but if you want to, that table is the whole of it.

Two warnings. It takes weeks before you can build these fluently yourself. And mnemonics are better evidenced for learning something than for keeping it — they give you a route back when recall fails. Spaced retrieval is still what holds it.
